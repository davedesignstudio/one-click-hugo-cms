Screws and biscuits have their place. For furniture that must stay square under weight and seasonal movement, few joints outperform a well-cut mortise and tenon.

## Strength where it counts

A tenon carries load into a mortise across a long glue surface and, when pinned or wedged, resists racking. Dining tables, bed frames, and workbenches rely on that geometry every day.

## Fit is the craft

The joint only works if the shoulders seat cleanly and the cheeks make even contact. We cut tenons slightly proud, then pare to a press fit—tight enough to assemble with light persuasion, never so tight that the wood bruises.

## Modern adhesives, traditional form

Today’s glues are stronger than the wood fibers around them. That doesn’t replace good joinery; it rewards it. A correctly sized tenon plus modern adhesive is a partnership, not a shortcut.

## When we use alternatives

Dominos, dowels, and screws appear where access, scale, or panel work call for them. The question is always the same: what load does this connection see, and how will it be repaired twenty years from now?
